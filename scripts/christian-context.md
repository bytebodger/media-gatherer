# SKEPTICUS SCRIPT: "The Context Card"

[INTRO]

There's one word that turns otherwise functioning adults into gibbering toddlers. It's not a long word. It's not a complicated word. It's seven letters. C-O-N-T-E-X-T.

Say "Leviticus" to a Christian apologist and watch what happens. You barely get the word out of your mouth. The reply comes flying back before your sentence even lands. "That's out of context."

You haven't quoted anything yet. You said one word. One book title. And somehow you're already guilty of contextual malpractice.

This happens every single time. I bring up something biblical. Doesn't matter what. Doesn't matter how directly I quote it. The "out of context" card gets played like it's the only card in the deck. Honestly, sometimes I think these guys learned the phrase phonetically. They can pronounce it. I'm not convinced they could define it under oath.

It doesn't even seem to matter whether the verse is famous or obscure. Quote something from the Sermon on the Mount, and somehow that's still out of context. Quote a one-line genealogy nobody's ever heard a sermon about, also out of context. At some point you start to suspect the word has stopped meaning anything at all. It's not a description of a textual problem anymore. It's a Pavlovian response, triggered by the sound of a Bible verse leaving an atheist's mouth.

So today, we're doing something radical. We're going to actually talk about what context means. Real context. Not magic-word context. Not "shut up, this is uncomfortable" context. We're going to walk through what the word legitimately requires. We're going to walk through what it doesn't require. And then we're going to land on the single biggest offender of contextual abuse on planet Earth. Spoiler. It's not atheists. It's Christianity itself. We've got a doozy of an example waiting at the end. Isaiah 7:14. Buckle up.

[POINT ONE: THE REFLEX]

Let's start with the reflex itself, because the reflex is the tell.

When someone reaches for "out of context" before they've even processed what you said, that's not engagement. That's a defense mechanism. It's the rhetorical equivalent of slamming your hands over your ears and humming.

Here's the thing about a real accusation of missing context. It requires work. You have to know what the full context actually is. You have to explain how that context changes the meaning. You have to show your work, like a math teacher demanded back in middle school.

Instead, what we get is a thought-terminating cliche. Three words. No follow-up. No explanation of what the supposed context even is. Just "out of context," dropped like a mic, conversation over.

I've started asking a simple follow-up question whenever this happens. "Okay. What's the context, then?" You'd be amazed how often that question gets met with silence. Or a deflection. Or a hasty Google search performed in real time while pretending it isn't happening. I can practically hear the typing through the screen.

Sometimes I genuinely wonder if these folks know what the word means. Not as an insult. As a real question. Because the way it gets deployed has nothing to do with the dictionary definition. It's used like a magic spell. Say the word, and the uncomfortable verse vanishes in a puff of smoke. No analysis required. No homework. Just incantation.

And look, I get why it's appealing. It's efficient. It ends the conversation instantly. It makes the other guy feel dumb for even bringing it up. But efficiency isn't the same thing as being right. A magic word isn't an argument. It's a shortcut around having one.

Let me show you what this actually sounds like in the wild. I'm not exaggerating this exchange. I've had some version of it more times than I can count.

Me: "1 Samuel 15 has God commanding the slaughter of Amalekite infants."

Them: "Out of context."

Me: "Okay. What's the context?"

Them: "You have to understand the historical situation."

Me: "Sure. What was it?"

Them: "It's complicated."

Me: "Try me."

Them: silence, or a link to a forty-five minute apologetics video that, spoiler, never actually addresses the verse either.

Notice what happened there. At no point did anyone explain how the context changes the plain meaning of the sentence. At no point did anyone even attempt to. The word got deployed, the conversation got redirected, and the actual verse never got revisited. That's not a contextual correction. That's a magic trick. Look over here while the rabbit disappears.

[POINT TWO: YOU DON'T NEED HEBREW]

Let's tackle the first big myth. The idea that you can't understand the bible's context unless you know Hebrew, Aramaic, or Greek.

Now look, I'm not going to pretend there's nothing to original language study. There is. Hebrew carries shades of meaning that don't always survive translation. Greek verb tenses can imply things that English flattens right out. If you're doing serious academic work, knowing the original languages helps a lot. Nobody disputes that. I'm certainly not going to stand here and pretend a translation captures one hundred percent of every pun, every wordplay, every double meaning baked into the original.

But here's what doesn't follow from that. The claim that an English reader, reading an English bible, cover to cover, is somehow incapable of grasping the basic context of what's being said.

Think about that claim for a second. Apply it to any other book. Apply it to "Crime and Punishment." Nobody tells you that you can't understand Dostoevsky unless you read it in the original Russian. You can absolutely get the plot. You can get the characters. You can track the themes. You're reading a translation, sure, and maybe some untranslatable joke gets lost in the margins. But the contextual shape of the story? You've got it. You read the whole thing, start to finish, in your own language.

The bible doesn't get some special exemption from how translation works. If a translated text conveys enough information for you to follow the narrative, the arguments, and the timeline, then you have the context. Full stop. That's true whether you're reading Tolstoy, Homer, or Leviticus.

And here's the part that really exposes the hypocrisy. Christianity spent centuries insisting that ordinary people should have access to scripture in their own language. That's a foundational achievement of the Reformation. William Tyndale was executed for trying to put an English bible into the hands of regular people. The whole project of putting a bible into every home, every church pew, every hotel nightstand drawer, rests on the assumption that English speakers can read it and understand it.

You don't get to have it both ways. You can't hand someone an English bible, tell them to read it for spiritual guidance, and then, the moment they actually read it and find something horrifying or contradictory, turn around and say, "well, you don't really get it, you needed the Hebrew." If the English translation isn't good enough to deliver the real meaning, then stop handing out English bibles. Pull every Gideon bible out of every hotel nightstand in the country. But if you're going to keep pushing the English translation as the literal word of God for English speakers, you don't get to retract that claim the second somebody reads Numbers 31 and asks an uncomfortable question about it.

You can't market the easy mode and then disqualify the player for using it.

And notice this argument never gets deployed consistently. Nobody tells a Christian who's comforted by Psalm 23 in English that their comfort doesn't count because they didn't read it in Hebrew. Nobody tells a Christian quoting John 3:16 from memory that the verse doesn't really mean what they think it means because the original Greek has some subtlety they're missing. The "you need the original language" card only ever gets played in one direction. It shows up exactly when the English text says something the apologist doesn't want it to say, and it disappears the instant the English text is doing rhetorical work they like. That's not a principle. That's a tell.

[POINT THREE: YOU DON'T NEED THE WHOLE CANON OPEN ON YOUR LAP]

Myth number two. The idea that you can't understand any given verse without cross-referencing it against completely different books, written in different centuries, by different authors, for entirely different audiences.

Again, there's a kernel of something real buried in here. Looking at how a word or theme develops across the bible can be genuinely interesting. Scholars do this. It's a legitimate exercise. Tracing how the concept of covenant shifts from Exodus to Jeremiah tells you something about a changing theology over time.

But that's an enrichment activity. It is not a requirement for basic comprehension. It's the difference between reading the footnotes for fun and being told you failed the assignment because you didn't read the footnotes.

If I read Genesis and form an opinion about what Genesis says, I am not required to first cross-reference every relevant New Testament passage before my reading counts as valid. Genesis was written, edited, and circulated as its own text, centuries before anyone had heard of Galilee. The idea that you need foreknowledge of a document that didn't exist yet in order to properly understand a document that did already exist isn't how reading works. It isn't how any text works, religious or otherwise.

This gets used as a shield constantly. I read a brutal verse from the Old Testament. The reply isn't "here's the surrounding verses in that very chapter that change the meaning." The reply is "well, you have to read it through the lens of the New Testament." That's not context. That's theology. Those are different categories of thing, and apologists smush them together because smushing them together is convenient. Theology is an interpretive lens applied after the fact. Context is what the words actually say, in their own setting, on their own terms. You're allowed to disagree with what a passage plainly says. You are not allowed to claim the passage doesn't say it just because a completely different book, written five hundred years later, takes a different position.

Think about how strange this would sound in any other field. Imagine reading the Code of Hammurabi and being told you can't understand its laws about property damage unless you first study the entirety of modern American contract law, written three and a half thousand years later, in a country that didn't exist yet, in a legal tradition the Babylonians never heard of. Nobody would accept that as a legitimate reading requirement. They'd recognize it instantly as an attempt to launder an ancient document through a much later framework that has no business being there. The bible gets a free pass on this exact move constantly, simply because the later framework happens to be a sequel that shares a publisher.

[POINT FOUR: WHAT KIND OF CONTEXT, EXACTLY]

Here's where we need some precision, because "context" isn't one single thing. There's historical context. Theological context. Cultural context. Authorial context. Literary context. The list goes on longer than most sermons.

When someone throws around the word without specifying which kind they mean, here's the deal. The default should be rhetorical context. Meaning, am I quoting a sentence or a phrase honestly, in line with how it functions in its own paragraph? Am I representing the basic flow of the passage accurately? That's the bar. It's not a high bar. It's the same bar we apply to every other piece of writing on Earth.

If I read a verse and quote it the way it's actually written, in line with the sentence before it and the sentence after it, I am reading in context. Rhetorically speaking, I've done my job. I haven't chopped a sentence in half to reverse its meaning. I haven't lifted three words out of a much longer thought and pretended those three words stand alone.

What apologists actually want, half the time, is something much bigger than that. They want you to absorb an entire theological framework before you're allowed to comment on a single verse. They want ancient Near Eastern cultural anthropology, centuries of denominational doctrine, and frequently a very specific interpretive tradition that just so happens to resolve the exact problem you raised.

That's not context. That's a moving goalpost wearing a context costume. And it's a convenient one, because the goalpost only ever moves when you've gotten close enough to score.

Watch how the standard shifts depending on the verse. Quote something that makes the religion look good, a comforting Psalm, a Proverb about kindness, and suddenly rhetorical context is plenty. Nobody demands you cross-reference ancient Near Eastern shepherding customs before you're allowed to appreciate "The Lord is my shepherd." But quote something that makes the religion look bad, and suddenly the bar rockets up to a full graduate seminar in comparative ancient Near Eastern law codes. The standard for "enough context" isn't fixed. It's whatever happens to be just out of reach of the verse currently embarrassing them.

Here's an analogy, since I spend my day job around code. Imagine handing someone a single function out of a massive codebase and they ask what it does. If the function is well written, they can usually tell you, just from reading the function itself. They don't need the entire repository open in twelve tabs. They don't need the commit history going back six years. Sometimes, sure, a function references something defined way upstream, and then yeah, you've got to go look at that. But that's the exception, not the rule. Most code, like most writing, is supposed to be comprehensible in the chunk you're looking at. If your text requires the reader to load the entire history of human theology into working memory before a single sentence makes sense, that's not a reader problem. That's a documentation problem.

[POINT FIVE: DON'T HAND OUT A TEXT YOU CLAIM PEOPLE CAN'T UNDERSTAND]

This leads to an obvious follow-up question. If a passage genuinely cannot be understood without an enormous, specialized body of outside knowledge, history, culture, theology, whatever flavor you want, then why is anyone handing this text to ordinary people and telling them their eternal soul depends on reading it?

You can't build a religion on personal bible study, on "just read it for yourself and you'll find God," and then turn around and gatekeep comprehension behind a seminary degree. If reading the text plainly leads to confusion, contradiction, or discomfort, that's not automatically a referendum on the reader's ignorance. Sometimes it's just a problem with the text. Sometimes it's a problem with how the text is being marketed to the public.

A doctor doesn't hand you a surgical textbook, tell you it'll heal your soul, and then call you stupid when you can't follow the terminology. Either the text is meant for general consumption, in which case general comprehension should be sufficient, or it isn't, in which case stop telling people to read it on their own and stop putting it in hotel drawers.

This is the part that always gets glossed over. Apologists want it both ways, simultaneously. The bible is simple enough that anyone can find salvation in it. The bible is also complicated enough that any layperson who reads it and finds a contradiction simply lacks the proper context. You cannot hold both of those positions at once. Pick one. Either the text is for everyone, or it requires a doctorate. It doesn't get to be both, depending on which answer is more convenient in the moment.

Watch how fast the framing flips depending on what's at stake. Ask someone how they know Jesus loves them, and the answer is often "I just read the bible." Simple. Direct. No translation committee required, no seminary, no footnotes. Ask the same person about a genocide commanded in Deuteronomy, and suddenly the bible transforms into the most layered, nuanced, context-dependent document in human history, comprehensible only to those with years of specialized training. It's remarkable how a book's difficulty level seems to scale precisely with how uncomfortable the passage in front of you happens to be.

You see this exact move with polygamy in the patriarchal narratives, or with the treatment of women throughout the law codes. Bring it up, and you'll get told you can't judge it without understanding "honor-shame culture," or "ancient Near Eastern marriage customs," or some other phrase that sounds impressive and explains nothing. Nobody offering that explanation ever finishes the sentence. They never actually walk you through how knowing about ancient marriage customs changes whether a specific commandment was right or wrong. The cultural context gets gestured at, vaguely, and then the conversation moves on, as if gesturing at it was the same thing as answering it.

[POINT SIX: WHICH PASSAGES ACTUALLY NEED CONTEXT]

Now, if you genuinely want to wield the Context Hammer with any credibility, you need an actual, consistent framework for which passages need broader context and which ones don't. Most apologists have no such framework. They swing the hammer based on whether the verse embarrasses them. Not based on any consistent principle about genre, structure, or literary form.

Some parts of the bible are narrative. A story is unfolding. Characters develop. Plot points build on earlier plot points. If you yank one sentence out of the middle of a long narrative arc and build an entire argument on that sentence while ignoring everything around it, sure, that's a fair complaint. A genuinely fair one. I'll grant that one without a fight.

But huge chunks of the bible are not narrative at all. They're law codes. They're proverbs. They're individual sayings, stacked like bricks, each one complete on its own.

Take Leviticus and its rules about clean and unclean animals. You don't need the entire book of Leviticus to understand what the text is commanding. It's a law. It states a rule. The rule is the rule. You don't need forty surrounding chapters to grasp "don't eat shellfish." A law-code doesn't function like a novel. Each statute largely stands on its own two feet, the way a single line in a legal code stands on its own without requiring you to read the entire municipal charter first.

Same goes for Proverbs. A single proverb is a self-contained nugget of wisdom literature. You don't need to understand Genesis, or the Gospels, or Leviticus, in order to evaluate a single proverb on its own merits. That's the entire genre. Standalone wisdom sayings. That's the design of the book, by definition. Nobody reading a fortune cookie demands the rest of the fortune cookie factory's catalog before they're allowed an opinion on the one they cracked open.

The Ten Commandments work the same way. Each commandment is a self-contained directive. "You shall not murder" doesn't require forty chapters of surrounding narrative to parse. It's a sentence. It says what it says. If you want to point out that the very same law code turns around a few chapters later and prescribes death for working on the Sabbath, you don't need anyone's permission to notice that, and you certainly don't need a seminary degree to flag the tension. The commandments were written as standalone legal statutes. They can be evaluated as standalone legal statutes.

So when someone quotes a brutal commandment from the Pentateuch, or a troubling proverb, and the response is "you're taking that out of context," ask them this. What, specifically, is the missing context? Not vague hand-waving about "the culture of the time." An actual textual context. The kind that would change what the sentence is plainly saying, if you read it. Nine times out of ten, there isn't one. They just don't like that the verse says what it says, and "context" is the closest thing they've got to a fire extinguisher.

[POINT SEVEN: THE WORST OFFENDERS ARE CHRISTIANS THEMSELVES]

Now here's the part where this gets genuinely funny, because irony doesn't get much richer than this. The group most prone to ripping scripture violently out of context, on a scale that would make a Genesis-quoting atheist blush with embarrassment, is Christians.

Exhibit A, and honestly the gold-medal champion of this entire category, is the so-called fulfilled prophecy. Christian apologetics loves to claim that Jesus fulfilled dozens of specific predictions made centuries earlier in the Hebrew scriptures. No example showcases the problem better than Isaiah 7:14.

Let's set the scene, because the scene matters enormously here, and it's the part that conveniently never comes up at church.

Isaiah chapter seven is set during the reign of King Ahaz of Judah. Ahaz is facing a real, immediate military crisis. Two kings, the king of Aram and the king of Israel, have allied against him and are threatening to conquer Judah and replace Ahaz with a puppet ruler of their choosing. Ahaz is terrified. Isaiah shows up with a message meant to reassure him, straight from God.

That message includes a sign. In the NRSV, Isaiah 7:14 reads, "Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Look, the young woman is with child and shall bear a son, and shall name him Immanuel."

Notice the wording there. The young woman. Not "a virgin." The original Hebrew word is "almah," and it means a young woman of childbearing age. Hebrew already has a specific, dedicated word for "virgin." That word is "betulah." It is not the word used in this verse. If the original author had wanted to specify virginity, the vocabulary existed, sitting right there, and it simply was not chosen.

But it gets better. Keep reading the chapter. The sign isn't framed as "a baby will be born one day, in some far-off messianic future, seven centuries from now." The very next verses tell you exactly when this sign actually matters. Isaiah says that before this child even reaches the age where he can tell right from wrong, both kings currently threatening Ahaz will be wiped out. This is a short-term, contemporary sign, delivered to address an immediate eighth-century-BCE political crisis. It's explicitly tied to events unfolding within the lifetime of a child born around the time the prophecy is spoken. The whole point of the sign is its speed. It's meant to comfort a panicking king right now, not promise a savior to his great-great-great-great-grandchildren's descendants.

This has nothing to do with a messiah arriving seven hundred years later. This is a wartime morale message for a frightened king, with a built-in expiration date measured in years, not millennia.

So how did this become "the virgin birth prophecy" that gets read aloud every December? Centuries later, when the Hebrew scriptures were translated into Greek, the translation we call the Septuagint, the translators rendered "almah" using the Greek word "parthenos." That word leans much harder toward "virgin" than the original Hebrew ever did. When the Gospel of Matthew was later written in Greek, for a Greek-reading audience, Matthew quotes this already-shifted, already-translated version of the verse to claim Jesus fulfilled it. Matthew 1:23, NRSV, reads, "Look, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall name him Emmanuel, which means, God is with us."

So here's the full sequence of contextual abuse, stacked up like a Jenga tower built entirely out of motivated reasoning. Step one, take a specific, time-bound, contemporary political prophecy addressed to a specific eighth-century king, about a specific eighth-century military crisis. Step two, ignore the surrounding verses that explicitly tell you the timeframe involved. Step three, rely on a translation shift, from "young woman" to "virgin," that the original Hebrew text never actually supports. Step four, rip the resulting line completely out of its narrative, historical, and linguistic setting. Step five, slap it onto an entirely different person, in an entirely different century, solving an entirely different problem, and call the whole mess a fulfilled prophecy.

And this isn't an isolated incident either. It's a pattern, and it runs through the whole "fulfilled prophecy" genre. Take Hosea 11:1. Read it in its own book, and it says, "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son." Read the rest of the sentence, and the rest of the chapter, and it's obviously about the nation of Israel, looking back at the Exodus from Egypt under Moses. There's no future child in view anywhere in the passage. It's a backward-looking line about a nation, not a forward-looking prediction about a person. Matthew 2:15 nevertheless lifts that single clause and applies it to the infant Jesus fleeing to Egypt with his parents, treating a sentence about a nation's past as though it were a prophecy about a baby's future.

Or take Psalm 22. Read the whole psalm, and it's a personal lament, a cry of anguish from someone in immediate distress, describing his own suffering and his own circumstances in his own first-person voice. It has an internal context. It's about the psalmist, addressing his own situation, to his own God. That hasn't stopped centuries of apologists from treating individual lines out of that very personal poem as a forensic, line-by-line itinerary of events at the crucifixion, written seven hundred years in advance, ignoring the parts of the very same psalm that don't fit the crucifixion narrative at all.

The pattern is always identical. Find an old line. Strip away its original setting. Repurpose it for a brand new narrative that its actual author never imagined and couldn't possibly have intended.

If an atheist did this to literally any other ancient document, treated one line from a specific historical text as a secret coded message about a future event seven centuries later, while ignoring the parts of the very same document that explain what the line is actually about, historians would correctly call that crackpot numerology. They'd laugh you out of the room. But do it to Isaiah, and it becomes the centerpiece of the Christmas Eve sermon, illustrated on greeting cards, set to music in carols sung by candlelight.

This is what makes the whole "you're taking it out of context" routine so rich with irony. The people slinging that accusation the loudest are sitting directly on top of the single biggest decontextualization project in religious history. Isaiah 7:14 isn't a gotcha I invented in my basement. It's sitting right there in the actual text, plain as day, contradicting the popular Christmas-pageant reading the second you bother to read the surrounding verses in the same chapter.

And here's the kicker. If you ever point this out to an apologist, watch what happens. Watch them reach, on instinct, for the exact same three words they've been hurling at you this entire video. "You're taking that out of context." Except now you can ask them the question right back, the one they never bothered to answer for you. What's the context, specifically, and how does it change anything? They read the whole chapter. The timeframe is right there in black and white. The Hebrew word is right there too. There's no hidden cultural key, no secret authorial intent, no ancient idiom that magically resolves the problem. The context is sitting in plain English, and it's the context that breaks their reading, not mine. Funny how the rule about reading the whole passage only seems to matter when it's pointed at someone else's argument.

[OUTRO]

So here's where that leaves us. "Context" is a real word, with a real meaning, and it doesn't mean "I don't like what you just quoted." It doesn't require a divinity degree. It doesn't require fluent Hebrew. It doesn't require cross-referencing six unrelated books written centuries apart from each other. Most of the time, it just requires reading the actual sentences surrounding the sentence you're discussing, in the language you're already reading, and representing them honestly.

And if you genuinely care about contextual integrity, the very first place to point that concern is at your own tradition's prophecy claims. Isaiah 7:14 isn't a gotcha I invented. It's sitting right there in the text, plain as day, contradicting the popular reading the second you read the surrounding verses.

Here's the test I'd love for every apologist to actually run on themselves, just once. Before you accuse someone of taking a verse out of context, write down what the context is. In a full sentence. Not a vibe. Not a category. An actual explanation of what surrounding information changes the meaning of the words on the page, and how. If you can do that, great, now we've got something to actually discuss. If you can't do it, you weren't defending the text. You were just defending your own discomfort, and you reached for the nearest three-word fire extinguisher to put it out.

So next time someone tries to wave away an uncomfortable verse with "that's out of context," ask them the only question that actually matters. What's the context, specifically, and how does it change the meaning? If they can't answer that question in detail, they're not defending scripture. They're reciting a magic word, and hoping you don't notice the magic was never real to begin with.
