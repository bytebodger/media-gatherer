In the year 325, the Roman Emperor Constantine summoned bishops from across the
empire to the city of Nicaea. The Council of Nicaea was the first ecumenical
council of the Christian church, and its purpose was to settle a dispute that
threatened to tear the young religion apart.

At the heart of the controversy stood a priest named Arius. Arius taught that
the Son was created by the Father, and therefore was not co-eternal with him.
Against him stood Athanasius of Alexandria, who insisted that the Father and the
Son were of the same substance.

The debate was not merely academic. To ordinary believers, candlelight flickered
in stone cathedrals as the argument raged over the nature of God himself. The
bishops argued for weeks in the summer heat.

In the end, the council produced the Nicene Creed, a statement of faith that is
still recited today. Arius was condemned and exiled, and his books were ordered
to be burned. But the controversy did not end there.

For decades afterward, the empire swung between the two positions. Emperors took
sides, bishops were deposed and reinstated, and the very definition of orthodoxy
remained contested. The question of what it means for something to be eternal —
uncreated, without beginning — is a genuinely difficult one, and no council vote
can make a hard idea simple.

Centuries later, in the cold libraries of medieval monasteries, scribes copied
these creeds onto parchment by hand, preserving a debate whose stakes they could
barely reconstruct. The ink they used has outlasted the empire that started it all.
